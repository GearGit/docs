## What a Pre-Trial Bias Survey Is

A pre-trial bias survey is a structured research instrument administered to a representative sample of the eligible juror population in the trial venue. Its core purpose is to assess whether community members hold pre-existing attitudes, toward the parties, the alleged conduct, the type of case, or the media coverage, that could compromise their ability to serve as impartial jurors.

The findings are used in two primary ways:

**Change of venue motions.** When the research establishes that a substantial proportion of the venue population has been exposed to prejudicial publicity and holds fixed opinions that voir dire is unlikely to rehabilitate, the results form the empirical foundation of a motion to transfer the case to a different judicial district.

**Jury selection strategy.** Even when a venue motion is not sought or is denied, the research documents attitudinal patterns in the community that shape voir dire questioning, juror profiling criteria, and peremptory challenge strategy.

The research is distinguishable from opinion polling and market research by the specific legal purpose it serves and the evidentiary scrutiny it must withstand.

## When Pre-Trial Bias Surveys Are Used

Not every case warrants a formal community attitude survey. The research is most commonly commissioned when one or more of the following conditions apply:

**High-profile criminal cases with significant media coverage.** When news coverage has been extensive, geographically concentrated, and emotionally charged (coverage of violent crimes, corporate fraud cases with identifiable victims, or matters involving public figures), the risk of community-wide bias is elevated and documentable.

**Civil cases with unusual local salience.** Major industrial accidents, environmental contamination cases, or lawsuits involving a dominant local employer can generate deep community attitudes even without national media coverage.

**Cases involving community figures or institutions.** When the defendant or plaintiff is a recognized local figure (a politician, law enforcement officer, religious institution, school, or large employer), the research may reveal that community ties are too strong to permit impartial evaluation.

**Cases being retried in the same venue.** When a mistrial or appellate reversal returns a case to the same jurisdiction, community attitudes established during the first trial may have solidified further, making a formal survey more likely to demonstrate lasting bias.

The decision to commission a community attitude survey should be made in consultation with trial counsel, weighing the cost of the research against the probability of a successful venue motion or the strategic value of the attitudinal data for voir dire.

## What Courts Typically Require

Courts evaluating community attitude surveys assess the research on several dimensions. While standards vary by jurisdiction and individual judicial discretion, the following factors consistently influence judicial reception:

**Methodological rigor.** The sampling method, sample size, and data collection procedures must be described in sufficient detail that a court can evaluate whether they conform to accepted social science practice. Ad hoc procedures or undocumented methodology weaken the research's evidentiary weight.

**Representativeness.** The sample must be drawn from or representative of the eligible juror population of the trial venue, not a convenience sample of online panel respondents with undefined geographic distribution. Courts are skeptical of samples that over-represent certain demographics or under-represent others in ways that could inflate bias findings.

**Neutral question design.** Questions must not lead respondents toward a particular answer. Courts will scrutinize the instrument for loaded language, order effects, or question framing that suggests the sponsoring party's preferred outcome. A neutral instrument demonstrates that bias, where it exists, exists independent of the survey's structure.

**Expert qualification and testimony.** The survey must be sponsored and testified to by a qualified expert, typically a social scientist, survey methodologist, or trial consultant with demonstrable credentials in this area. The expert must be prepared to defend every methodological choice under cross-examination.

**Statistical competence.** Margins of error, confidence intervals, and cross-tabulation results must be correctly calculated and clearly reported. Courts increasingly expect these elements as baseline requirements.

## Step-by-Step Methodology

### Step 1: Define Geographic Scope

The survey must be administered within the potential juror pool of the trial venue. Defining this scope involves:

- Identifying the judicial district from which jurors will be drawn
- Determining whether the relevant unit is the county, multi-county district, metropolitan statistical area (MSA), or some other defined geography
- Consulting the court's jury plan or local rules for the geographic boundaries of the jury pool

If a venue motion is the objective, the scope may also include the proposed alternative venue, to compare attitudinal distributions and demonstrate that the alternative venue is more favorable to a fair trial.

Geographic scope must be documented precisely and consistently applied throughout data collection.

### Step 2: Determine Sample Size

Sample size is a function of the desired margin of error, the confidence level, the estimated prevalence of the attitude being measured, and the level of cross-tabulation the analysis will require.

General guidance for community attitude surveys:

- A sample of 300 to 400 completed responses from eligible respondents provides a reasonable starting threshold for a simple proportional analysis at the venue level.
- When the research must report results separately by demographic subgroup (by age cohort, by racial/ethnic group, by education level), the total sample must be large enough for each subgroup to meet minimum cell size requirements for statistically interpretable results.
- When cross-tabulation by multiple simultaneous variables is required, samples of 600 to 1,200 completed responses are more defensible.

These are general planning thresholds, not legal requirements. A statistician should determine the sample size for each specific project based on the analysis plan.

### Step 3: Sampling Method

Courts are more receptive to surveys that can demonstrate a credible claim to representativeness. Best practice involves:

**Random sampling as the baseline.** Respondents should be drawn from or screened to represent the eligible juror population, not recruited from convenience panels without controls.

**Stratified sampling with demographic quotas.** The sample should be stratified to match the venue's eligible juror population on key demographic dimensions: typically age, gender, race/ethnicity, and education. Quotas enforced during data collection ensure the completed sample reflects the population distribution.

**Eligibility screening.** Before any substantive questions are asked, the instrument must verify that respondents meet the basic criteria for jury eligibility in the venue: age, residency, citizenship, and absence of prior felony conviction.

**Documentation of sampling method.** The expert report must describe the sampling approach in enough detail that it can be replicated and defended. Panel sources, quota targets, screener criteria, and response rates (or completion rates) should all be documented.

### Step 4: Survey Instrument Design

A well-structured pre-trial bias survey moves from general to specific, establishing case awareness before probing for opinion, and gathers demographic information in a consistent format. The instrument typically includes the following sections in this order:

**Case awareness: unaided recall.** Before naming the case, parties, or underlying events, probe for spontaneous awareness: "In the past year, have you seen, heard, or read anything about \[general event category\] in \[county/region\]?" This establishes whether respondents have been exposed to case-related information without priming them with specifics.

**Case awareness: aided recall.** After the unaided probe, introduce identifying information about the case (typically through a neutral factual description) and assess recognition and recall depth: "Have you seen, heard, or read anything about this matter? If so, where did you first learn about it?"

**Media exposure battery.** Assess the volume, source, and recency of media exposure. Questions should cover television, online news, social media, and interpersonal discussion separately, both because courts attend to media saturation and because exposure source may moderate opinion formation.

**Opinion and attitude questions: neutrally worded.** These are the core attitudinal measures. Questions must be neutrally worded, with balanced response scales, and must not lead respondents toward a finding of bias. Typical measures include:

- Opinion about guilt or liability before the trial
- Perceived fairness of a trial in the venue
- Ability to set aside prior knowledge and decide the case on the evidence
- General attitudes toward the type of defendant or plaintiff (institutional, individual, corporate)

**Difficulty of setting aside prior opinion.** Courts pay particular attention to questions that assess not just whether respondents hold an opinion, but whether they believe they could set it aside. The proportion of respondents who express doubt about their own impartiality is often the most probative finding in a venue motion.

**Juror eligibility screeners.** Screeners for jury eligibility should appear at the beginning of the instrument to route out ineligible respondents before they encounter substantive questions.

**Demographic battery.** Age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, county of residence, employment status, and any other variables relevant to cross-tabulation should be collected at the close of the instrument, after the substantive questions. This ordering prevents demographic salience from influencing responses to attitudinal questions.

### Step 5: Data Collection and Quality Control

Data collection procedures directly affect the defensibility of the results:

**Mode.** Online surveys are the most common mode for community attitude research. When online panel sources are used, geographic filtering and quota controls must be applied rigorously to ensure respondents are drawn from or match the eligible juror population of the specific venue.

**Quality control.** Duplicate submissions, speeders (respondents who complete the survey in implausibly short times), and straight-liners (respondents who select the same answer for every question) should be identified and removed before the dataset is finalized. Document the quality control procedure and the number of responses removed.

**Timing.** Data collection should be documented by date and time. Collecting data over an extended period in a rapidly evolving news environment creates temporal confounds. The community's attitudinal distribution may shift if major new coverage emerges mid-collection.

**Response-level data preservation.** Each completed response should be preserved at the individual level, with timestamps, device or IP identifiers (where legally permissible), and the full response record. This data is the foundation of the expert report and must be available for disclosure.

### Step 6: Analysis

The analysis moves from descriptive summaries to cross-tabulations to statistical significance testing:

**Awareness and exposure rates.** Report the proportion of respondents who demonstrate unaided and aided awareness of the case, and the depth and source of media exposure.

**Opinion distributions.** Report the distribution of attitudinal responses (the proportion who believe the defendant is guilty or liable, the proportion who believe a fair trial is impossible, the proportion who express doubt about their own impartiality) with confidence intervals.

**Cross-tabulations.** Examine whether awareness and opinion levels vary by demographic subgroup. Findings that show high bias levels across all demographic segments are more compelling than findings that are concentrated in a single subgroup.

**Statistical significance.** Report whether observed differences, between the survey venue and a comparison venue, or between demographic subgroups, are statistically significant. Do not report percentages without the associated confidence interval or margin of error.

**Comparison venue analysis.** If the purpose of the research is to support a venue motion, a parallel survey administered in the proposed alternative venue provides a statistical basis for the comparison courts require.

### Step 7: Expert Report Preparation

The expert report translates the data into a declaration or report suitable for submission with a motion. It must:

- Describe the expert's qualifications
- Explain the purpose and design of the research
- Document the sampling method, sample size, and demographic composition of the completed sample
- Present the findings with appropriate statistical detail
- Offer expert opinions, grounded in the data, about the implications for the fairness of a trial in the venue
- Attach the survey instrument, the full dataset (or a representative sample), and the methodology documentation as exhibits

The expert must be prepared to testify to every element of the methodology under direct examination and cross-examination.

## Question Design Principles

The most common point of attack on community attitude surveys is the survey instrument itself. Opposing counsel and judicial scrutiny focus on whether the questions are neutral, whether the order creates demand characteristics, and whether response scales are balanced.

**Neutrality.** Questions must not suggest a preferred answer. Avoid value-laden descriptors (e.g., "brutal crime" versus "the alleged incident"), partisan framing, or language that implies one party's account is accurate.

**Balanced response scales.** Likert-type scales should be symmetric, an equal number of options on each side of a neutral midpoint, unless there is a methodological reason to depart from this. Asymmetric scales create scale-direction bias.

**Funnel order: general before specific.** Begin with general awareness and general attitudinal questions before introducing case-specific content. This reduces priming effects and makes the unaided recall findings more credible.

**Avoid double-barreled questions.** Questions that ask about two things simultaneously ("Have you seen news coverage and formed an opinion about this case?") cannot be cleanly interpreted and should be split.

**Pre-test the instrument.** Before administering the survey in the field, conduct a cognitive pre-test with a small number of respondents, asking them to think aloud as they answer, to identify questions that are misunderstood, ambiguous, or leading in ways that were not intended.

## Common Methodological Flaws That Undermine Court Acceptance

Courts and opposing experts have developed a consistent list of methodological criticisms that recur across venue motions. Anticipating these flaws during instrument design and data collection is more effective than trying to address them in rebuttal testimony.

**Non-representative samples.** Using an online panel without geographic controls or demographic quotas produces a sample that does not represent the eligible juror population. Courts can fairly ask: "How do you know this sample reflects the community?"

**Leading questions.** Instrument questions that describe the case in loaded or one-sided terms, or that frame the attitudinal probe in a way that suggests a bias-finding is expected, give opposing counsel an easy line of attack.

**Inadequate screeners.** Failing to verify jury eligibility before administering substantive questions means ineligible respondents are contributing to the bias findings. An opposing expert will challenge the composition of the dataset.

**Absence of statistical controls.** Reporting raw percentages without confidence intervals or margins of error suggests methodological naivete and makes statistical cross-examination more difficult.

**Inconsistent geographic targeting.** If the data collection spanned multiple counties, states, or postal code ranges that extend beyond the jury pool, the representativeness claim is weakened.

**Undocumented data collection procedures.** Inability to produce timestamped response-level data, documentation of quality control steps, or a record of who had access to the data creates an audit trail gap that opposing counsel will exploit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a pre-trial bias survey?

A pre-trial bias survey is a structured community attitude study administered to a representative sample of the eligible juror population in the trial venue before jury selection begins. It measures whether community members hold pre-existing opinions about the case or parties that could compromise their ability to serve as impartial jurors. The research is used to support change of venue motions or inform jury selection strategy, and must satisfy the methodological standards courts apply to survey evidence.

### When should a trial consultant commission a pre-trial bias survey?

A pre-trial bias survey is most warranted when there is reason to believe that pretrial publicity or community ties have created widespread attitudes that voir dire alone cannot identify or rehabilitate. High-profile criminal cases, civil cases involving local institutions or prominent figures, and cases being retried in the same venue following a mistrial are common situations where the research is appropriate. The decision should weigh the cost of the survey against the probability of a successful venue motion or the strategic value of attitudinal data for voir dire preparation.

### What makes a pre-trial bias survey methodologically defensible?

Courts evaluate community attitude surveys on four primary dimensions: representativeness of the sample (does it reflect the eligible juror population?), neutrality of the instrument (are questions free of leading language?), statistical rigor (are margins of error and confidence intervals reported?), and expert qualifications (is the sponsoring expert credentialed in survey methodology or social science?). Surveys that fail on any of these dimensions are vulnerable to opposing expert critique and judicial skepticism.

### How many responses are needed for a community attitude survey?

Sample size depends on the margin of error required, the level of cross-tabulation the analysis will include, and the size of the venue population. A simple proportional analysis may be defensible with 300 to 400 completed responses from eligible jurors. When the expert report will present cross-tabulations by multiple demographic variables, 600 to 1,200 completed responses are typically needed to achieve statistically interpretable results at the subgroup level. A statistician should determine the specific sample size requirement for each project.

### How should questions be ordered in a pre-trial bias survey?

Questions should move from general to specific, establishing awareness before probing for opinion, and collecting demographic information at the end of the instrument. Begin with unaided awareness probes, before introducing case-specific details, then progress to aided awareness, media exposure, attitudinal questions, and the eligibility screener. Demographic questions appear last to prevent demographic salience from influencing earlier responses. This funnel structure reduces priming effects and makes the unaided recall findings more credible to courts.
